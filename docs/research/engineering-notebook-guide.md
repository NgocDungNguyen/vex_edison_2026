# Award-Level VEX IQ Engineering Notebook Plan: Level Up 2026-27 (GRSF Rubric 2.0)

> Research report written 2026-11-03 for Team Zenith, reusable for every team in this repo.
> Official rubric PDFs are in [`../rubrics/`](../rubrics/). Notebook templates are in [`../templates/`](../templates/).
> **Caveat:** this report was written before the rubric PDFs were available, so the exact 16 criterion names and the 1-4 level wording are not in it. Check against the PDFs.

The biggest thing to know is that the rubric changed this season. The old RECF rubric (11 criteria, 0-5 points each, 55 maximum, with a 5-point bonus for showing the notebook was kept in sequence) no longer applies. This season's notebooks are scored on the Global Robotics & Science Foundation (GRSF) Engineering Notebook Rubric 2.0: 16 criteria, each scored 1-4, for 64 points. Three quarters of those points (48 of 64) reward a design process the team repeats, tests, and reflects on. Organization earns the rest. The team also needs to submit three new one-page summaries (Season, Code, Credit) with the notebook, and every word must be written by the students. No adult and no generative AI may write any of it.

## TL;DR

- **Official criteria (GRSF, August 2026):** five sections. Notebook Organization is worth 16 points, Define 12, Develop Solutions 16, Optimize 12, Reflection & Iteration 8. Each criterion is scored 1-4. Judges treat 48 or more as a useful sign that a notebook is "Fully Developed", but GRSF tells them to "treat 48 as a benchmark rather than a rule". A Fully Developed notebook is required for the Design Award, and the Excellence Award requires every Design Award criterion plus top-40% competition rankings.
- **What wins:** several real design cycles, each running from problem to brainstorm, decision, build or code, measured test, and change based on the data, across the shooter, intake, arm, drive, and autonomous. Show failures honestly. Credit any outside ideas, including ideas from AI tools. The notebook, the one-page summaries, the robot, and what students say in the interview should all tell the same story.
- **Vietnam specifics:** the Vietnam VIQRC MS National Championship takes place January 23-24, 2027 at Dwight School Hanoi. Notebooks are submitted digitally and interviews happen in person. The event page gives the notebook deadline as 8:00 PM on January 10, 2027 (the Vietnamese text says "2026", almost certainly a typo). Paper or digital format is set by each Event Partner, and both are scored identically.

## 1. Who sets the rules now

In May 2026, VEX Robotics split from the REC Foundation and set up the Global Robotics & Science Foundation (GRSF) to run its competitions. RECF is now running its own separate programs. For VEX IQ Level Up, the official judging documents are therefore the GRSF Library pages (library.globalrobotics.org) and VEX's own pages (kb.vex.com, events.vex.com, manuals.vex.com). The Vietnam National Championship event page itself tells teams to go to library.globalrobotics.org for the latest award descriptions and judging criteria.

Much of the online advice still quotes the 2025-26 RECF Guide to Judging. That includes the 55-point rubric, the 5-point "in-sequence" bonus, and the rule that a notebook is Fully Developed if it scores at least 2 on the first four criteria, which still appears on RECF's own kb.roboticseducation.org page. Treat all of that as out of date for VEX IQ Level Up. Some of the underlying good practice still holds: dated entries, no editing old entries after the fact, and showing failures.

The Level Up game manual was Version 2.1 (released October 8, 2026) when this was written. Main points:

- Bean Bags measuring 3"x4" are scored into Goals, and higher Goals earn more points.
- A robot may hold or plow only one Bean Bag at a time.
- Teamwork matches last 60 seconds, played by a two-robot alliance.
- There are Driving Skills and Autonomous Coding Skills matches.

Check the manual version and the Q&A before every event. Official Q&A answers count as part of the manual.

## 2. Official judging criteria

### 2.1 Engineering Notebook Rubric 2.0 (GRSF, dated August 6, 2026)

| Section | Points (% of 64) | What judges look for (official GRSF wording, paraphrased) | Award-level evidence for the team |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Notebook Organization** | 16 (25%) | "whether a reader can find things, tell who wrote what, see that the record was kept across the season, and see where outside ideas came from" | Table of contents, page numbers, dates, team number, student initials on every entry, outside sources credited where they are used |
| **Define** | 12 (18.75%) | "the problem, the criteria and constraints around it, the team's scoring plan, and the ideas it considered" | Level Up game analysis, constraints from the manual (size, one-bag possession limit, motor and sensor limits), a scoring plan with trade-offs, several distinct brainstormed concepts |
| **Develop Solutions** | 16 (25%) | "prototypes, test procedures, recorded results, and how the team explained its decisions" | Ideally three or more connected prototypes per subsystem, repeatable test procedures, data tables, decision matrices |
| **Optimize** | 12 (18.75%) | "how mechanisms and code are documented, and whether changes trace back to test results" | Annotated photos, sketches or CAD, hardware/port maps, code documentation, each change linked to a specific test |
| **Reflection & Iteration** | 8 (12.5%) | "whether the team reflected as it went, and whether it moved through the design process more than once" | Reflections after every test session and every event, with several full design-process cycles across build, strategy, and code |

**Scoring mechanics**

- Every criterion is scored 1-4. A submitted notebook gets at least 1 on each, so totals run from 16 to 64.
- Judges rank all notebooks and pick out the strongest group. At a typical event that is "roughly the top ten", and these are the pool for the Design, Innovate, and Excellence Awards.
- GRSF tells judges that "A score of 48 or above is a useful guide to determine if the notebook is Fully Developed", because 48 means the notebook averaged Proficient across all 16 criteria. It adds: "Where the cut falls at your event depends on what was submitted, so treat 48 as a benchmark rather than a rule."
- "Fully Developed" means the notebook is one of the event's top notebooks and has examples of each Engineering Design Process category: Define, Develop Solutions, and Optimize.
- Rubric scores are only a sorting tool. Final awards are decided in discussion among the judges. GRSF says two notebooks a single point apart "are not meaningfully different".

**Gap:** the exact names of the 16 criteria and the wording for each 1-4 level were not available when this was written. Use the official PDF in `docs/rubrics/` and the Student-Friendly version (stars and self-check questions, designed for students to check their own notebook, not used by judges).

### 2.2 What judges are told about reading notebooks

- **Start with the Season Summary.** Judges read it first and follow its page pointers into the notebook. GRSF notes that a notebook "can run past a hundred pages, and the summary exists so you do not have to find the substance yourself".
- **The summaries are not scored.** "A thin or missing summary is not a deduction". In practice, a good summary decides which pages a judge actually reads.
- **Polish doesn't count.** "A legible working notebook scores as well as a polished one", and paper and digital "are scored identically".
- **Age is taken into account.** Judges score a young VEX IQ team on the process it actually followed, at its own level of sophistication, and not against the best notebook in the room.
- **Judges may not use AI tools** to review, summarize, score, or screen notebooks. That includes AI detectors such as GPTZero.

### 2.3 Design Award vs. Excellence Award (GRSF Award Descriptions)

| | Design Award | Excellence Award |
|---|---|---|
| Recognizes | "an organized, student-driven Engineering Design Process through its Engineering Notebook and Team Interview" | "overall excellence in both judged and performance categories", the highest honor at an event |
| Notebook | Must be a Fully Developed notebook that "ranks among the strongest at the event" (required) | "Meets all criteria of the Design Award, including a fully developed Engineering Notebook and a strong Team Interview" |
| Other criteria | Iterative, student-driven EDP; "Clearly explains robot design, strategy, and decision-making" | Also must finish in the top 40% of teams in the Qualification Rankings and the Robot Skills rankings (use final rankings). In 2025-26 there was also an Autonomous Coding Skills threshold; **not confirmed for 2026-27** |
| Related | Innovate Award requires meeting all Design criteria. Think Award (coding) requires taking part in Autonomous Coding Skills | Events with judging must offer the Excellence, Design, and Judges Awards |

The notebook is the entry ticket for both awards. Excellence adds robot performance on top. Teams with an autonomous program should run Autonomous Coding Skills at every event. That keeps them eligible for the Think Award and protects their standing in the Skills rankings for Excellence.

### 2.4 Team Interview Rubric 2.0

- Six criteria in two sections:
  - **Explain Your Learning** (6 points): whether students can explain their own work, trace how they got there "(including what did not work)", and show how they know their robot works.
  - **How You Worked With Others** (6 points): whether students learned with each other, made decisions together, and made outside ideas their own "by crediting, adapting, testing, and understanding them".
- Each criterion is scored Heard with Specifics (2), Heard (1), or Not Yet Heard (0), for 12 points total.
- To earn a 2, the answer must come with concrete detail: "a named part, a number, a source, a moment from the season".
- "A memorized speech, however polished, will not score well." Students may consult their notebook during the interview "the way engineers reference their own documentation", and "No one student has to explain everything".

## 4. What makes a notebook award-level

### 4.1 Official guidance (GRSF / VEX)

- **Define:** explain the criteria and constraints ("the rules and limits that could impact possible solutions"), and document the game strategy "including the scoring priorities and tradeoffs". Record "several distinct approaches during brainstorming".
- **Develop Solutions:** document continuous prototyping, "ideally three or more attempts at a solution that clearly connect to one another". For every test, say what was measured, how, and under what conditions, so someone else could repeat it. A simple table works (Trial # | Variable | Result).
- **Optimize:** "Make sure changes to the robot or code follow clearly from your recorded test results, with the connection between testing and changes made explicit."
- **Reflection:** "Reflect throughout the season, not just at the end", and show iteration in building, strategy, and coding "so a judge can see a continuous pattern rather than a single change".
- **Authenticity:** leave in misspellings, failed ideas, and real problem-solving. Don't rewrite old entries for a particular event.
- **Templates:** VEX publishes official 2026-27 Level Up Digital Engineering Notebook templates in Google Slides and PowerPoint, with instructions, field maps, calendar pages, and digital VEX parts images. The IQ template now includes the AI Vision Sensor. They are a good starting point and follow the Student-Centered Policy.

### 4.2 Community advice (unofficial, but common among strong teams)

- **Page cross-references.** If you split the notebook into sections (intro, engineering, programming, weekly log), use page callbacks such as "continued from p. 23" and "data on p. 31". (VEX Forum, inspired by 21417A's notebook.)
- **Keep the designs you rejected.** Several Forum posters say the designs you didn't pick, and the things that failed, are some of the most valuable pages.
- **Be clear about calculations.** Judges and notebookers on the Forum report that "Calculations" is the most loosely interpreted area. For IQ, calculations that tie clearly to decisions are enough: gear ratios and output RPM, shooter success rate (%), cycle time, expected points per match.
- **Name the problem twice.** A Judge Advisor suggested defining both the *game* problem and the *robot design* problem, because judges read "problem definition" in different ways.
- **Hand sketches are fine at IQ level.** CAD is welcome but not required. Whether IQ needs CAD was not answered by GRSF.

## 5. Technical sections for a robot like Zenith's

Zenith's robot: drive base (two drive motors), two-motor shooter, intake motor, arm motor, distance sensor (shooter-loaded state), Touch LED (status LED and autonomous start button), driver-control and autonomous programs.

1. **Hardware configuration table (one page, updated with each version):** Port | Device | Name in code | Purpose. Add the gear ratios and note any motor reversed in code.
2. **Controller mapping diagram:** a drawing of the IQ controller with each button or joystick labeled (drive, intake in/out, shooter spin-up/fire, arm up/down). Record *why* each control was placed where it is, using driver feedback, and include any changes made after practice.
3. **Distance sensor "shooter loaded" study:** the problem (the driver can't see whether a bag is loaded), the thresholds tried (for example 20/40/60 mm), and a trial table showing false positives and negatives for each threshold, with the chosen value and the reason. This is a model Develop -> Optimize cycle.
4. **Touch LED state table:** each colour or blink means a state (for example ready / loaded / auto-armed / error), and pressing it starts autonomous. Explain why a physical start button was chosen and how it was tested.
5. **Two-motor shooter:** concepts brainstormed (flywheel vs. catapult vs. puncher), decision matrix, speed or velocity tests against goal height, success rate per 10 shots, and the iterations.
6. **Autonomous routine:** field map with the planned path, a flowchart of the steps (start on LED press -> drive -> intake -> check distance sensor -> shoot -> ...), pseudocode, a run-by-run consistency log (for example 10 runs, points each), and the changes made. Keep a version history table: version | date | change | reason (test #) | result.
7. **Driver-control code:** a description of the control loop, any toggles or state logic, and how the sensors feed the LED.
8. **Code Summary (one page):** pull this from items 6-7 in plain language so a non-coder can follow the logic, and list any outside code in the Credit Summary. If code is printed, include only important snippets with comments. There is no need to include every revision.

## 6. Common mistakes that lose points

| Mistake | Why it hurts (rubric area) | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Writing entries after the fact ("catch-up" pages the night before submission) | Organization: "record kept across the season"; reads as curated | Write the entry in the last 10 minutes of every session. If an entry is late, label it "written on [date] about work on [date]" |
| Missing dates, page numbers, author initials | Organization (16 points is the cheapest section to lose) | Use a page header template and check it with the checklist below |
| Only successes shown | Reflection & Iteration; a notebook with no setbacks looks curated | Keep a "What failed / why / what next" box on every test page |
| Tests without numbers or a repeatable method | Develop Solutions | State the variable, method, conditions, and trials, and present the data in a table |
| Changes not linked to data | Optimize: "changes trace back to test results" | Every change entry should cite the test that triggered it ("Based on Test 7, p. 34...") |
| Copied manual text or other teams' content | Wasted pages; uncredited outside ideas break the Student-Centered Policy | Summarize rules in the students' own words and credit sources where they appear and in the Credit Summary |
| Adult-written, adult-"cleaned up", or AI-written content | Breaks the GRSF Student-Centered Policy: "No adult and no generative AI tool writes it for them." | Students write everything. AI may be used to learn, but AI-sourced ideas must be credited |
| Robot in the notebook doesn't match the robot at the event | Judges check that the notebook, interview, and robot agree | Make an "As of event" entry with photos and the current port map before each submission |
| Too long and unfocused | Judges have limited review time | Strong Season Summary with page pointers; move repetitive material to appendices |

**How a mentor helps without owning the notebook (GRSF Student-Centered Policy):** mentors can teach skills such as how to build a decision matrix or a data table. "A good mentor asks more than answers." If a mentor does step in, "the help stays brief, the mentor names it out loud, and the work goes back to the student." In practice:

- Ask questions such as "What did you measure? What will you change? Which test proves it?"
- Never write or type in the notebook.
- Hold a short student-led notebook review each week, using the Student-Friendly rubric.

## 7. Templates

**Every page header:** Team <number> <name> | Page # | Date | Title (include the design-process stage, e.g. "DEFINE - Shooter problem") | Authors' initials | Students present.

**A. Design Process entry:** Problem/goal -> Criteria and constraints (with manual rule numbers) -> Brainstorm (at least 3 labeled sketches) -> Decision matrix -> Chosen solution and why -> Build or code plan -> Next test.

**B. Decision matrix (example for a shooter):**

| Criterion (weight) | Flywheel | Catapult | Puncher |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy to high goal (x3) | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Reload speed (x2) | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Motor use (2 motors available) (x2) | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Build complexity (x1) | 3 | 3 | 2 |
| **Weighted total** | **29** | **23** | **23** |

Scores 1-4 are given by the students, followed by a sentence on why the winner won and what risk remains.

**C. Test / iteration log:** Test # | Purpose | Method and conditions (battery %, distance, field position) | Variable | Trials table | Average or success % | Analysis | Change decided -> link to build or code entry.

**D. Meeting log (short):** Goals for today (SMART) | Who did what (roles) | Done / not done | Problems | Plan for next meeting | Timeline status (Gantt or calendar check).

**E. Programming entry:** Goal -> Flowchart or pseudocode -> Key snippet with comments -> Version number -> Test runs table -> Bugs found and fixes -> Credit for any outside code.

**F. Game analysis:** Field map with dimensions; scoring table and points per goal height; rules that limit the design (possession limit, size, preload, driver switch); match timeline; strategy options with expected points; chosen strategy and trade-offs; a separate Skills strategy (Driving and Autonomous Coding).

**G. Competition reflection:** Event, date, ranking and Skills scores; what worked; failure analysis (with match numbers); interview takeaways; top 3 changes -> each one becomes a new Define entry. (First-time teams can reflect on practice sessions too, not only competitions.)

### Recommended starter outline

1. Cover (team number, name, school, season)
2. Table of contents
3. Meet the team and roles (update when roles change)
4. Season goals and timeline
5. Engineering Design Process explanation (in students' own words)
6. Level Up game analysis and strategy
7. Robot v1 design cycles by subsystem (drive, intake, shooter, arm, sensors)
8. Programming (configuration, controller map, driver code, autonomous)
9. Competition reflections
10. Iteration cycles v2, v3...
11. Current robot "as of event" page
12. Appendices (data, extra photos) and drafts of the Season, Code, and Credit Summaries

### Mentor's pre-judging checklist

- [ ] Every page has a page number, date, title, and author initials; the TOC is up to date; the team number is on the cover
- [ ] At least 3 full design cycles (Define -> Develop -> Optimize), with at least one in code (for example the autonomous or a sensor threshold)
- [ ] Every subsystem has at least 2 brainstormed alternatives and a decision rationale or matrix
- [ ] Every test has a method, numbers, and an analysis, and every change cites a test
- [ ] Failures are documented with lessons learned; there is a reflection after each event
- [ ] The port/config table, controller map, and LED state table match the robot as it is today
- [ ] Outside sources (Hero Bot Flop, videos, other teams, AI-sourced ideas, template code) are credited in place and in the Credit Summary
- [ ] The Season Summary points to page numbers; the Code Summary can be read by a non-coder; both are student-written
- [ ] For digital submissions: the link opens without logging in, everything is inside the document, and it is submitted before the event deadline
- [ ] Each student can explain at least two notebook sections they wrote

## 8. Linking the notebook to the interview

Judges read the Season Summary before the interview and then ask the team to take them "underneath" a highlight. They also look for the notebook, interview, and robot to agree. Each student should own a part of the robot and be ready to answer the following, with a number or a page reference:

- What problem were we solving, and which rules limited us?
- What alternatives did we consider, and why did we pick this shooter or intake design?
- What did a test show? For example: "Our distance-sensor threshold gave X% false triggers at 40 mm, so we changed it to Y (p. Z)."
- What failed, and what did we change?
- How does our autonomous decide what to do (sensor checks, LED start)? Explain it from the Code Summary.
- How do we know the robot works (consistency data)?
- How did we make decisions as a team, and where did outside ideas come from (credited)?

Practise with mock interviews using the Student-Friendly Interview Rubric. Avoid memorized speeches. GRSF says they "will not score well".

## 9. Step-by-step plan (to January 2027)

1. **Now:** print the GRSF notebook and interview rubrics (official and student-friendly) and the VEX 2026-27 IQ digital template. Hold a team session on the Student-Centered Policy. Confirm the deadlines and format with each event organizer, including the 2026 vs. 2027 typo for Nationals.
2. **Choose the format:** Nationals requires digital submission, so a shared Google Slides or Docs template (with automatic revision history) is the lowest-risk choice. If the team prefers paper, scan it to PDF.
3. **Assign roles:** one notebook lead to keep the structure consistent, but every builder and coder writes their own entries and initials them.
4. **Backfill honestly:** add the Game Analysis and v1 robot pages now, clearly labelled with today's date and "reconstructed from photos and memory of work on [date]".
5. **Every session:** spend the last 10 minutes on entries using templates A-E. Write test pages for the shooter, the sensor, and the autonomous first, because they give the best Develop and Optimize evidence.
6. **After each event:** write a competition reflection (template G) that becomes the next Define entry.
7. **Two weeks before submission:** students self-score with the Student-Friendly rubric and fill gaps with genuine new entries, not rewrites of old ones. The mentor runs the checklist above and only asks questions.
8. **One week before:** students write the Season, Code, and Credit Summaries. Run a mock interview. Check that the link works when logged out.
